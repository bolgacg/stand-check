Before anything else

Five things to know

This page is about the log a test stand writes, so here is what that log is and what can go wrong in it. Nothing after this assumes you have read one.

  1. A test stand's data acquisition system writes one row per sample: a timestamp and one number for each channel, such as a pressure transducer, a thermocouple, a load cell, or a valve's command and its feedback.
  2. A channel map says what each channel is, its unit, and whether it must be live. Every map here is written from the publisher's own README, overview or column headers, never from the checker's output.
  3. Three kinds of fault leave every value looking normal: dropped samples (rows that never arrived, or that the logger filled by repeating the last row), a stuck sensor (a channel that stops changing), and a valve mismatch (the valve's electrical output, position or downstream pressure does not follow its command).
  4. A range limit flags a channel whose value leaves the range it showed in the stand's other runs. Most stands have one, so it is the comparison on this page.
  5. Every result is given per site: one record (the channels that share one clock), one channel, or one valve, in one run.

Terms, in the order they appear
planted fault A fault I wrote into a copy of a real run, at a known site, time and size, so the count of what a check catches is exact.
false alarm A flag on a site that has nothing wrong with it.
hotfire, cold flow A test that burns propellant, and one that only flows fluid through the plumbing.
record The rows a logger writes on one clock. A run can have several, at different rates.
live, spare A live channel is one the documentation names, so it must carry a signal. A spare one is reported for information only.
flat Exactly the same value on consecutive samples. Real sensors carry noise, so a long flat stretch means something stopped.
driver output The voltage a valve driver puts on a solenoid when commanded on. Here, on means at least three quarters of battery voltage, off at most half.
setpoint The position a motorised valve is told to go to, logged beside the position it reports.
saturated A sensor or amplifier that has reached the top of its range and reads flat there while the real value keeps rising.
fit and measured runs Thresholds were set on the fit runs only; the measured runs were checked once with them frozen. Act two and the coda say which is which.

Limits, stated before you find them

It cannot see a sensor that freezes but keeps its noise. A blocked pressure port does this: the transducer keeps reading the trapped pressure plus its electrical noise. Catching it needs a second sensor at the same point to compare against, as the DLR rig has at each station, or a check of the pressure against the valve sequence. This checker has neither.

It missed the saturated dynamic sensors in the DLR tests. They sit flat at their top for , and the stuck check waits on fast channels. A check for a channel flat at its own maximum would catch them. I saw a research note about these flat tops before fixing the thresholds and chose not to tune for them, so the miss is reported as measured.

Valve timing on the Delft bench is only as good as its valve log. The valve states carry no timestamps and no documented rate, and their record length disagrees with the thermocouple record by up to percent in one run. A valve that answers half a second late cannot be told apart from that drift.

Three stands is a small sample. The thresholds are per channel type, not per stand, and a new stand would need its own fit runs.

This is a post-test check in Python and JavaScript. It is not a real-time interlock, not PLC logic and not LabVIEW, and it reads files after the test rather than signals during it.

The range limit here is a stand-in. A real stand's limits come from its test plan. Mine come from the stand's other runs, which suits a range limit as well as any data-driven choice can, but it is still my choice.

Changes made after the measured runs were checked

Three, all listed with reasons in PLAN.md: planting faults only where neither check already flags; starting a planted valve fault where the valve check starts looking; and counting the range limit on channels only, so one out-of-range channel is not counted three times. None changed a threshold.
